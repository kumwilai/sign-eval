This page is documentation, not the study. It reproduces every screen and every participant-facing string of the Thai human-evaluation instrument used in this paper, with an English translation beside each Thai original, so that a reviewer who does not read Thai can check what participants were actually asked. This page collects nothing, stores nothing and sends nothing: it has no scripts, no forms and no network requests. The live instrument is at https://kumwilai.github.io/sign-eval/.

What the participants were asked

ประเมินท่ามือ

Rate the signing

The instrument’s own title, as it appears in the browser tab and as the first heading a participant sees. The Thai says “evaluate hand postures”, not “evaluate sign language”: the task never asks the participant to judge language content.

The instrument is entirely in Thai because the participants are Thai Sign Language users. Since 2026-09-09 the instrument is delivered as a personal link emailed to each participant (?p=NN) and they complete it alone: no researcher is present, no one observes the session, and there is no interpreter, so every word of substance has to reach the participant through the screen. The link is now binding, not a convenience: the number in it is the session’s identity, the field on screen is read-only, and a page opened without a link refuses to start rather than offering the next free number to whoever arrived. A link opened a second time also refuses, on a screen of its own, instead of moving the person quietly onto somebody else’s number. Everything below is text the screen itself shows. Strings that the page assembles at run time are given exactly as they appear in the source, with the code’s own placeholders left in place, and an English rendering with the placeholders filled in for a complete session.

Facts a reviewer needs to read the screens

Every number here is read from the instrument source (he_logic.js, he_markup.html) or from the stimulus kit’s provenance.json, not from a protocol document.

Screens in a complete session
10 items × 5 rated conditions = 50 single-clip trials, plus 1 unscored practice trial, plus 10 forced-choice pairs, plus 10 reference-fidelity pairs = 71 screens. The on-screen progress bar counts the 70 scored screens; the practice trial is outside it.
What the participant is told a condition is
Nothing. Each clip is named only by an opaque blind code (sk_001…sk_060), and the published page source contains no condition label at all — only two roles, the anchor clip (practice clip and fidelity reference) and the pair the forced choice compares. Codes are rejoined to conditions after the fact from a blind map that stays on the researcher’s machine. The five rated conditions are GT, GOV, REF, RCX and PT; no participant and no screen ever sees those names.
Replay budget, as the constants stand
MAXPLAYS_SINGLE = 3 and MAXPLAYS_PAIR = 4, counting the automatic first play. A single-clip screen therefore allows 2 replays and a pair screen 3 replays; on a pair screen one replay tap restarts both clips together, so the cap counts screens watched rather than video elements started. The used count is recorded per trial as plays. The welcome screen’s own summary of the budget disagrees with these constants — see the flag on screen 1.
Active-time cap
LIMIT_MS = 40*60*1000 — 40 minutes of active time. Time spent on the break screen, on the two instruction screens, and with the tab hidden is credited back to the deadline, so a long rest or a phone call does not consume the session. A countdown appears only inside the last five minutes (WARN_MS = 5*60*1000) and turns red under one minute. Reaching the cap ends the session as complete rather than as a failure. The welcome and consent screens tell the participant to expect about 20–25 minutes, which is the expected pace, not the cap.
Byte-identical control pairs
Three of the ten forced-choice pairs are the same file twice, so any preference on them is purely side bias. They are the three items on which the two compared conditions render identically: 06April_2010_Tuesday_tagesschau-306, 24October_2009_Saturday_tagesschau-4289 and 28October_2009_Wednesday_tagesschau-4543. Independently confirmed for this walkthrough by hashing the delivered MP4 files: the two blind codes for each of those three items have the same sha256, and no other item does. Their placement is drawn from a fixed admissible set, so a control is never the first pair and two controls are never closer than three screens apart.
A rendered condition that is never rated
Sign-IDD. Ten Sign-IDD clips exist in the stimulus kit and are never shown to a participant. The kit records why: the authors released no PHOENIX checkpoint, so the clips come from an unreproduced local reimplementation, and rating them would measure the reproduction rather than the published method. The kit holds 60 rendered clips; 50 are shown.
One rated condition is a frame-rate floor anchor
PT is released at 12.5 fps and played at its native rate, while every other condition is 25 fps. Its smoothness and clarity ratings are therefore confounded with the 80 ms frame hold; the kit records it as a floor anchor and a channel check, not as a confirmatory contrast.
Order, pacing and locking
Trial order is generated from a seeded generator, seed = 18964103 + participant index, so a session is reproducible from its participant number. The break comes after 25 rated single trials (BREAK_AFTER = 25). Answer buttons stay locked until every clip on the screen reports that it has ended, with an GATE_MS = 8000 safety unlock if a clip never reports it. Once the last answer on a screen is tapped the page advances itself after ADV_MS = 700 ms.
Participant codes
Assigned by the researcher, 1–99, displayed as P01…P99. Codes from 90 up (TEST_FROM = 90) are dry runs: every row they write carries test:true and a persistent banner marks the screen, so a rehearsal cannot be mistaken for data.
Recorded per rated single-clip trial
Written to ratings/: participant, pidx, session, test, item, code (the blind stimulus code), round, order, the four ratings natural, smooth, clarity, connected, interrupted (the tab was backgrounded during this trial), plays, changes (how many times an answer was changed), rt_ms, the per-scale reaction times rt_natural_ms, rt_smooth_ms, rt_clarity_ms, rt_connected_ms, elapsed_ms and at (ISO timestamp). Each per-scale time runs from the clip loading to that scale’s tap, net of time the tab was hidden; rt_ms is the largest of the four, that is, the time to the last answer on the screen. The practice trial writes no rating row: its answers are kept only inside the session document, under practice.
Recorded per forced-choice pair
Written to choices/: participant, pidx, session, test, phase:"choice", item, left_code, right_code, duel1_side (which side carried the second of the two compared conditions), choice ("left" or "right"), control (true on the byte-identical pairs), order, interrupted, plays, changes, rt_ms, elapsed_ms, at.
Recorded per fidelity pair
Written to fidelity/: participant, pidx, session, test, phase:"fidelity", item, code, gt_code, gt_side, order, fidelity (the 1–5 rating), interrupted, plays, changes, rt_ms, elapsed_ms, at.
Also recorded
A clip that fails to decode is never rated: the screen is skipped and a row goes to skips/ naming which element and which channel failed (reference or candidate on a fidelity screen, left or right on a forced-choice screen). One session document per session carries the consent flag and time, the seven background answers, the participant-code claim, which transports the data left by, the planned and completed counts per phase, the seed, the retest items, the replay caps, the practice answers, the skip list, the active/break/instruction/hidden time totals, the build id, the user agent, the viewport and the reduced-motion preference.
Which written design the instrument implements
Amendment 4 of the protocol: five conditions fully crossed within participant, four single-clip scales, a no-tie forced choice, and a fidelity phase. A reviewer comparing these screens with section 5 of the protocol body should know that the body as written still describes three rating scales, a four-way German-sentence meaning choice, and a preference task with a tie option; the instrument has four scales, no meaning-choice task, and no tie. Those three differences are amendment changes, not translation losses.

How to read the two columns

On a wide screen the Thai original is on the left and the English translation on the right. On a narrow screen they stack, with the English marked by an amber rule. Above each pair, a small line names the interface element the string belongs to — heading, lead paragraph, bullet, button, label, hint, counter, caption, badge, status or error — because the same words mean different things in a heading and on a button. An em dash in the Thai column means the screen shows no Thai text at that point — a digits-only counter, or an interaction that produces no message.

Placeholders such as ${n} are the source code’s own; they are left in the Thai exactly as written so a reviewer can find the string in the source, and the English gives the sentence with a real value substituted. Translations are literal in preference to fluent. Where a Thai phrase carries something an English reader would otherwise miss, or where the Thai is genuinely ambiguous, a note follows the pair; notes about a possible substantive mismatch between the screen and the written protocol are marked in a stronger colour and begin with the word Flag.

One register note applies throughout. The instrument addresses the participant as ท่าน, a formal, deferential “you” — more distant and more respectful than everyday Thai คุณ, and closer to the tone of a printed notice than to speech. It is translated below as plain “you” everywhere, so the politeness of the original is flatter in English than in Thai. Thanks and apologies end with the paired particles ครับ/ค่ะ: those are the male and female forms of the speaker’s politeness particle, written together because the page cannot know who is speaking. English has nothing corresponding, and they are not translated.

1Welcome, participant code, test-run toggle

The first screen, on the participant’s own device. The emailed link carries their assigned number as ?p=NN; the field is filled from the link and is read-only, and the test-run checkbox is set from the same number and locked with it, so there is nothing on this screen an unsupervised participant can get wrong and nothing that can move the session onto another number. Opened without a link the page fills nothing, disables the button and asks the participant to use the link from their email. When the link has already been used (2026-09-09)

A whole screen, reached instead of the first clip when the participant number in the link has already begun a session. It replaces a silent renumbering: the earlier build moved the person onto the lowest free number, wrote a one-line notice into the welcome card — which is hidden by that point, so nobody ever saw it — and ran them through another participant’s counterbalancing, because the whole trial order is seeded from the number. A reload, a flat battery, closing and reopening the tab, a second tap on the emailed link or a second tab were all enough to trigger it, since the number is recorded as used in the first second of the first attempt. The session now stops here. Nothing is rated, no claim is written, and one session record is filed under the same number with a different session nonce, so the researcher can see the revisit next to the first attempt and decide what to do about it.

What this page leaves out, and why

  • The stimulus videos. None is embedded. This document is about what participants were asked, not what they were shown, and embedding clips would also make it large and no longer offline-portable.
  • The delivery endpoint. The address the instrument posts rows to is in the source but is not reproduced here, and neither is any credential; nothing on this page can reach any server.
  • The blind code to condition mapping. Which of sk_001…sk_060 is which method is held in a file that stays on the researcher’s machine, so that the published instrument cannot leak it. The five rated condition names are given in the facts panel; the per-clip mapping is not.
  • The instrument’s own visual design. The screens are dark, single-column and phone-shaped, with one video stage and large tap targets. This page reproduces their text, not their layout, and does not attempt to be a screenshot.
  • The invitation email. Each participant receives a personal link, https://kumwilai.github.io/sign-eval/?p=NN, by email. That email, the written consent sheet and the researcher’s script are separate documents and are not reproduced here. Nothing is explained to the participant in person or in Thai Sign Language, which is why the note under the five points names an email address for questions. The written consent sheet is no longer among the documents a participant does not see: since 2026-09-09 the whole of it, sections 1 to 10, is on the consent screen itself, and screen 2 above reproduces its label, its contents section by section, and every place where its wording had to be changed to stay true of unsupervised delivery. The researcher’s script and the invitation email are still separate documents and are still not reproduced here.
